Why governance manuals fail in their first week

Most of the manuals I have seen did not fail because they were badly written. They failed because whoever wrote them never asked the people who would use them: what do you do when a decision lands on your desk and you don't know who owns it?

In recent years I have worked on manuals for ministries, organizations and companies. Almost every time, there was an earlier manual, printed and approved, that nobody opened.

Three reasons that keep coming back

  1. The manual describes the institution as it should be, not as it is.
  2. Nobody owns updating it once it is approved.
  3. New staff don't know it exists.
A rule that staff cannot explain to a colleague is a rule they will not apply.

What we did instead

We started from real decisions, not from the org chart. We collected fifty decisions made in the previous six months and asked about each one: who actually made it? And who should have?

42 pages9 pages and one matrix
Read only at hiringOpened at every decision
